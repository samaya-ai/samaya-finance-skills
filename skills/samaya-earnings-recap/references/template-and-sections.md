## THE TEMPLATE — fixed, and the same for every company and every sector

**The layout below is settled. It is the same for every company, every sector and every market.**
The content changes. The shape does not. A European staples half-year, a US quarter and a Japanese
full year all come out in this order, with these sections, numbered this way.

**Masthead strip.** House label and document type on the left, company, ticker and currency on the
right, on every page.

**Event strip.** One line above the title: the period, the months it covers, the release date and
the call date.

**Title and date.** The company, the period and the document type, with the preparation date
underneath.

Then, in this order and no other:

**VERDICT** — the takeaway, specified below.
**01 What changed on the call, and when it gets tested** — table.
**02 Key numbers** — table.
**03 Segment and brand performance** — charts and tables.
**04 Key debates from the earnings call** — table.
**05 Change in management narrative** — takeaway, then positives, then negatives.
**06 Further due diligence** — sensitivities and questions for management.
**Sources** — numbered list.

**Six pages is the target and the ceiling.** Nothing is added to this set and nothing is dropped
from it. Where a company gives nothing on a section, the section still appears and says so in a
row. Where it gives a great deal, the section is cut to fit rather than pushing another one off
the page. Anything elected beyond the default set displaces something.

**Every section heading carries superscript source numbers** for the sources that section draws
on, per the citations section below.

**No section or table is split across a page break.** A heading on one page with its table on the
next is a layout failure, not a rounding problem. Where a block will not fit in the space left,
the whole block moves to the next page. The same holds inside a table: a table that must break
repeats its header row, and a table that would break with two rows left is moved whole.

## THE VERDICT — the block at the top, on every run

**This is the same block on every run, for every company, in every market.** It is what makes the
page read like a person wrote it rather than a system filled it in.

**One sentence, then up to three bullets.**

The sentence carries the verdict: what the print did, which line is the story, and the figure that
carries it. The bullets carry what the call added that the release did not, one per point, ending
on the next dated test.

Written as a message to a client who reads twenty of these a day:

- **Say which line is the story, in words.** The guide is the story, or the margin is, or the call
  is. Say it before giving numbers.
- **Quantify the line that is the story. Dismiss the ones that are not, in a few words.** Where the
  margin came in where the street had it, "margin was fine" does the job and the table carries the
  figure.
- **Signs, not words.** Write `-3.5%`, never minus 3.5%. Same in every table.
- **Chat shorthand is correct here**: VA cons, cons, FY26, H2, bps.
- **State the figure and the comparison. Do not write beat or miss** where the table shows the
  difference.
- **Margins and growth rates in basis points.**
- **No em dashes.**
- **Plain contrasts are fine** — partly the brand itself rather than just sell-in; holds at this
  year's level rather than recovering. That is not the banned "X, not Y": it says both things
  rather than asserting a contrast for effect.
- **Read it aloud.** If it sounds like a document, rewrite it. If it sounds like a message, it is
  right.

Worked example, as the voice rather than a form to fill:

> Light on top line (-3.5% organic vs VA cons at -2.7%), but margin was fine and guide is the
> story (cut to at least 11.8% from 14.0% last year). Call added the bit the release left out:
> CEO conceded NIVEA decline is partly the brand itself rather than just sell-in · CFO said 2027
> margin holds at this year's level rather than recovering · Q3 sales on 27 October is the first
> clean read on whether sell-in converges with sell-out now the European retailer deals cover 92%.

## 01 — WHAT CHANGED ON THE CALL, AND WHEN IT GETS TESTED

One table, carrying what changed, why it matters and the date it gets tested. Columns:

| Theme | Said before, and when | Said on the call, and when | Why it matters | Next test |

- **Both quotations verbatim and short**, attributed to speaker role and dated.
- **Why it matters is the analyst's read**, not a restatement of the quote. Where it is a number
  management did not give, label it as an implication.
- **The next test column carries a date in the row**, with the event and what it settles.
- **Four to six rows.** Beyond six it stops being page one.

## 02 — KEY NUMBERS

**Percentages and margins, not currency actuals.** After the print the buy side works in growth
rates and margins; the reported sales figure has already been seen and does not need repeating
here. Carry organic growth, margins, the earnings figure and the guidance lines. Currency amounts
appear only where the line has no percentage form, such as an absolute profit figure that anchors
a margin.

The table has two blocks with their own sub-headings:

- **Reported** — the period's lines, each with the company figure, consensus and the difference.
- **Guidance** — the guide now in force, with **the previous guide shown underneath in lighter
  type**, so the change is visible in the cell rather than described below it.

Rules:

- **The consensus column header names the provider and the vintage date.** Not consensus: the
  provider, and as of when.
- **Differences in basis points for margins and growth rates, percent for absolutes**, with
  neutral wording — 14bps below, 1.5% ahead, 140bps below consensus. Never beat or miss.
- **Where a line has no consensus, say so in the cell** — not applicable at half-year, or not
  retrievable — and keep the row.
- **Colour carries direction**: ahead of consensus in green, below in red, with a one-line key.
- **At most two sentences above the table**, and they must account for every headline line,
  including the ones that went the other way. A sentence that names what was ahead and omits what
  was behind is a partial summary and is worse than no sentence. **Never claim a comparison the
  table does not show.**
- **One footnote under the table** at most, for the consensus vintage and any derived figure.

## 03 — SEGMENT AND BRAND PERFORMANCE

Two charts, each with a takeaway line above it and a table underneath carrying the same figures.
Charts are drawn from the retrieved figures, never from a separately recalled series.

- **Chart one: the trailing comparable periods.** Organic growth on the left axis and margin on the
  right, across the last four comparable periods. Same half or same quarter throughout, never
  mixed.
- **Chart two: growth by segment and brand.** The reported period against the shorter period
  inside it where the company gives both, so the reader sees where the quarter reads better or
  worse than the half.
- **The table under each chart repeats the plotted figures** plus the margin per segment where it
  is disclosed.
- **Where a split is not disclosed, say so in one line and do not construct it.** Price, volume and
  mix by brand is the usual case.
- **Where a figure the company gave before is missing this time, say so** and give the earlier
  figure with its date. A rate withdrawn in a period where the trend turned is part of the
  disclosure.

## 04 — KEY DEBATES FROM THE EARNINGS CALL

Columns: the debate, the brokers who raised it and how it was handled, and what was actually
given.

- **Ordered from answered most fully to least**, with those answered least fully grouped at the
  foot of the section.
- **Handling in neutral wording only**: answered, partially answered, not answered. Nothing that
  grades the answerer.
- **Brokers named by firm**, never individuals, and only where the transcript identifies them.
- **What was given is the substance of the answer**, not a characterisation of it.

## 05 — CHANGE IN MANAGEMENT NARRATIVE

**A takeaway line, then positives, then negatives**, each as a table:

| Theme | Said before | Said now | What it implies |

- **This section is exhaustive and is not forced into categories.** What counts as a narrative
  shift differs completely between a staples company, a semiconductor company and a bank, and any
  fixed set of buckets will fit one and distort the others. Carry every shift that has a before
  and an after.
- **Order by materiality inside each half**, so a reader who stops after three rows has the three
  that matter.
- **The takeaway above the tables carries the whole section in two sentences** — what the narrative
  moved from and what it moved to. A reader who reads only that has the section.
- **Said before carries its date and speaker role. Said now carries the call date.**
- **What it implies is one clause of analyst read.**

## 06 — FURTHER DUE DILIGENCE

- **Sensitivities management named**, each in one line, drawn from the call and the release.
- **Questions for management: five, no more.** Chosen as the ones that would most change the read,
  weighted toward what was asked and not answered.

## CITATIONS — superscript markers and a numbered source list

**Sources are numbered and cited as superscript markers. Whole sentences are never hyperlinked.**
A link under a sentence pulls the eye out of the line and breaks the reading; a small number after
the phrase does not.

- **A numbered source list at the foot of the document**, each entry naming the document and its
  date.
- **Superscript numbers on each section heading**, listing the sources that section draws on.
- **Superscript numbers inside the body only where a specific figure or quotation needs one that
  the section-level marker does not cover.**
- **One number per source per section.** The same source is not cited repeatedly inside a section.
- In the workbook, sourcing stays as Notes with their links, unchanged.

## NOT IN THIS VERSION

Two things belong in the next version and are named here so they are not silently dropped: a
consensus evolution chart showing how estimates moved around the event, and a market reaction
section covering the share price move on the print and on the call. Neither runs by default now.

## THE SURPRISES IN FULL — the section behind the page-one table

**The qualitative material is where the money is, and prose is where it goes to die.** A line
like a diagnosis being restated from market-driven to self-inflicted is worth more than any
figure on the page, and buried in the fourth paragraph of a narrative section it will not be
read. Lift it into a table.

One row per focus item, plus a row for any theme management raised unprompted. Five columns, and
each row is tagged positive or negative so it ties back to the page-one split:

| Theme | Said before, and when | Said now | The shift | What it implies |

- **Last quarter and this quarter are verbatim and short** — a clause or a sentence, dated and
  attributed to speaker role, not a paraphrase. The reader is judging the words, so give them
  the words.
- **The shift column names the change in three or four words** — hardened, softened, quantified,
  dropped, reframed from external to internal cause, conceded.
- **What it implies is one clause**, and it is the analyst's read rather than a restatement.
  Where the implication is a number management did not give, say so.
- **Where nothing shifted on a focus item, the row still appears** and says so. An unchanged
  narrative under a changed print is itself a finding.
- **Above the table, one sentence: how the overall language changed.** That sentence is a
  candidate for the headline and frequently is the headline.

Where the transcript does not support a comparison — a new speaker in the role, a theme not
raised last quarter — say so in the cell rather than reaching back two quarters silently.

## THE TAKEAWAY — the paragraph itself

Not a paragraph on this skill. Four labelled parts, in this order, the whole block
fitting the lower half of page one:

**What changed during the call.** Two or three sentences on what a listener knows
now that the release did not give — a new forward statement, a decomposition, a
concession, a commitment. Where the release did the work and the call added
reasoning only, say that outright in the first sentence: it is a finding about the
event, not a shortfall in the report. Where nothing changed, say nothing changed.

**Key numbers.** Three to five lines, each a number and its consequence, not a
restatement of the print — where the period landed against consensus, where the
guide now sits against consensus, and any figure derived on the call that moves
the shape of the remaining year. A number with no consequence attached does not
earn a line.

**Narrative change.** One or two sentences: how management is now framing the
business against how they framed it on the prior call, and whether the framing
hardened or softened. Where the frame is unchanged, say so.

**What to act on.** Two or three lines: the theme answered most fully and the theme
not answered, each named with the verdict against the question asked, and the dated
event that settles what the call left open.

Each part carries its label as a heading so the reader can take one and leave the
rest. Written last, from the body. The delta against the release is the point of
the whole block — a takeaway that restates printed numbers has not debriefed the
call. No rating, no target, no recommendation.

**The takeaway and the conclusion are two different blocks and are never merged.**
The takeaway is page one and it is what the reader acts on. The conclusion is the
last thing in the document and it closes the argument. A single block labelled
"Takeaway / Conclusion" at the end of the report means this rule was broken: the
reader reached page thirteen before finding the answer, which is the exact failure
the architecture exists to prevent. If only one of the two can be written, it is
the takeaway, on page one.
