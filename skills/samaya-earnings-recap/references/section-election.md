# What the company is judged on, and section election

Moved out of `SKILL.md` to keep it lean; read it at kickoff, before presenting the menu.

## Contents

- STEP 0c
- STEP 0d

## STEP 0c — WHAT THIS COMPANY IS JUDGED ON — established before anything is built

**Every company has two to four things the market actually judges it on, and they are not the
same two to four for the next company.** A personal-care business may be judged on price
position, the reinvestment funding it, and whether operating margin holds while that happens. A
beauty business may be judged on growth by category and by geography, and on nothing else in the
same quarter. A packaged-food business may be judged on one category and one geography. Report
the generic income statement to a reader who wanted the pricing-versus-reinvestment question and
the report has failed even where every figure in it is right.

**Establish the focus items first, from evidence, and state them.** Two to four, never more,
identified from:

- **What management themselves lead with** in the release and in the prepared remarks — order of
  presentation is a disclosure.
- **What the question-and-answer actually concentrated on**, counted by separate questioners
  rather than by minutes spoken. The buy side asks about what it is underwriting.
- **The KPIs the company chooses to disclose**, especially any introduced or dropped this period.
- **What moved the shares at the last two prints**, where retrievable, and what was blamed at the
  time.

Where the analyst names the focus items at invocation, use theirs and say so. Where the evidence
points two ways, carry both and say which the call weighted. **Name the focus items in one line
near the top of the report** so the reader knows what the debrief decided to be about, and
**every default section below leads on them** before it covers anything else.

This is not a section of the report. It is the decision that shapes every section.

## STEP 0d — SECTION ELECTION — run the defaults, then say what else exists

Where the invocation resolves a company, **run the default sections
immediately.** Do not hold the run to present a menu.

Print one line above the body — `Default set: <sections>. <n> optional sections
available, listed in the appendix.` — and build. The not-run list goes in the
appendix, never at the top.

Present the menu and wait only where one of these holds:

- the company or the period cannot be resolved from the invocation
- the request is ambiguous between this skill and a named neighbour
- the run is expensive: a window beyond the default, more than one name, or
  Full depth
- the user asks what the skill can do

Where the user names a section or a sub-section, build exactly that and nothing
else. Do not add sections back for completeness, do not append omitted sections,
and do not widen a single-item request into its parent section.

**Defaults (1 to 6) — these run without being asked for, and they are all that runs by default**

Each leads on the focus items from Step 0c. **Page one is section 1 in full and nothing else** —
see the page-one specification below, which governs where its parts sit and in what form.

1. **The recap** — page one, one page, four blocks: the biggest surprises, the key numbers, the
   key debates and how management responded, and the next catalyst. Specified below.
2. **The surprises in full** — every shift behind the page-one table, with the verbatim on both
   sides and the implication drawn out.
3. **What the call added** — everything said on the call that was not in the release: new
   disclosure, themes management raised unprompted, a number put on something the release left
   as a floor, a commitment made, a launch deferred. **If it was said on the call it belongs
   here, whether it came in the presentation, the prepared remarks or an answer.** The split
   between script and speech is a production detail, not an analytical one. Where the manner of
   delivery is itself the finding — something that hardened between script and answer, or was
   volunteered before anyone asked — say so in the line.
4. **Who pressed what** — themes ranked by the number of separate questioners who raised each, as
   a table, with answer quality scored against the question actually asked. Attribute to broker,
   never to an individual, and separate analysts from journalists where both were on the line.
5. **The numbers** — the focus items with reported, consensus and the guide in force side by
   side and the difference in its own column, how consensus moved after the event, any derived
   figure with its arithmetic shown, and segment, brand and geography detail **only where it says
   something the release did not**. A table that restates the print in the report's own
   formatting has cost a page and said nothing.
6. **What to watch next** — dated catalysts, each mapped to the focus item or open question it
   tests; the sensitivities management named; and three to six questions for management drawn
   from what was deflected or answered thinly.

Everything else is optional and runs only when elected. **Electing an optional section does not
extend the report** — it displaces appendix space or replaces a default that was not wanted.

**Optional (7 to 22)**

8. **Tone and hedging** — certainty language against the trailing three comparable
   calls, not only the immediately prior one; hedges added or removed, and where
   specificity was traded for generality.
9. **Speaker split** — who answered what, and whether the split changed. Where a
   speaker is new to the role, note it and do not read drift across the change.
10. **Consensus revision path** — how estimates moved in the days after the call,
    where retrievable.
11. **Share price reaction** — the move on the day and the following session,
    reported as a fact with no causal claim attached.
12. **Peer read-across** — what the call implies for named peers, built only from
    statements about shared end markets, inputs or channels.
13. **Analyst positioning** — which brokers asked which questions, by broker and
    not by individual. Requires the firm's own research entitlements to identify
    brokers reliably; where a broker cannot be identified, say so.
14. **Capital allocation on the call** — buyback, dividend, leverage and deal
    commentary as given.
15. **Cost and input commentary** — pricing, inflation, hedging and phasing as
    stated.
16. **Multi-quarter theme trace** — a named theme tracked across the last four
    calls with dated verbatim pivot points.
17. **Verbatim appendix** — short quotations on the elected themes, each dated
    and attributed to speaker role.
18. **Script structure and emphasis** — what led, what was buried and what was dropped against
    the prior call. Elect this where the ordering itself is a disclosure; the content that came
    out of the remarks sits in default section 3 regardless.
19. **Segment detail** — each reporting segment decomposed, with the driver split where
    disclosure supports it and geographic detail where given. Where the company does not disclose
    a driver split, say so rather than constructing one.
20. **Market action** — the share price move on the print and on the call, against the sector and
    against the prior four events, with a small chart. Elect where the move is itself in
    question. Planned as a page-one block in a later version and deliberately not one yet.
21. **Guidance after the call in full** — the revised or reiterated guide verbatim, phasing and
    the implied second-half shape, and where the new guide sits against consensus. The headline
    guidance change belongs in the read; this is the detail behind it.
22. **Trailing comparable periods, the guide in force, and what the print invited that nobody
    asked** — each useful occasionally and none of them reliably. Elect them where the trend, the
    guide history or the unasked question bears on the focus items; do not run them by habit.

Build only what is elected.
